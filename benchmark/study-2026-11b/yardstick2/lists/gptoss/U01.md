1. **Database schema change** – Adding an `emailVerified` column and related token tables requires a migration; if the migration fails or is applied inconsistently, existing user data could become corrupted or inaccessible.  

2. **Login logic regression** – Modifying `login.tsx` to reject unverified accounts may unintentionally block legitimate users (e.g., existing accounts without the new flag) and could introduce a silent failure path if the verification check is omitted or mis‑wired.  

3. **Email verification token security** – Storing or transmitting verification tokens must use cryptographically secure generation and expiration; weak tokens or long‑lived links could be guessed or abused to hijack accounts.  

4. **Email delivery reliability** – The new verification flow depends on external email delivery; bounced or delayed emails could lock users out, creating a denial‑of‑service risk and requiring robust retry/resend mechanisms.  

5. **User experience & edge cases** – New users who never receive the verification email or lose access to it need a safe “resend” or “change email” path; lacking this can lead to abandoned registrations and support overhead.  

6. **Potential enumeration attacks** – Exposing “account not verified” vs “invalid credentials” messages can allow attackers to discover which emails are registered; responses must be uniform to avoid leaking user existence.  

7. **Impact on existing authentication flows** – Password‑reset, account‑deletion, and OAuth‑based login may assume a verified email; introducing the flag without updating those flows could cause inconsistent states or security gaps.  

8. **Compliance and data privacy** – Storing verification timestamps and tokens introduces personal data that may be subject to GDPR/CCPA; appropriate retention, deletion, and audit logging must be ensured to avoid regulatory violations.
